The law set aside

Eleven times,the law gave way.

A conviction is not only a question of evidence; it is a question of law. Each departure below is stated with what happened, the specific authority it violated, what that law actually requires, and the strongest reply the other side could make.

How to read this page

Four parts, every time.

Each of the eleven sections follows the same order, so a reader can check every claim:

  1. What happened, told from the transcript or the decision, with the page beside every quotation.
  2. The authorities the court departed from: the Constitution, the Manual for Courts-Martial, the Articles of War, the Rules of Court and Supreme Court decisions.
  3. What the law requires, in plain words.
  4. What the other side may say, and the answer. A fair case meets its strongest objection.

The first two sections concern the sentence and the court's power. They need no weighing of evidence. The rest concern how the evidence was gathered, admitted, shut out and judged.

01

Punished for a crime that was never charged

The court ruled that torture was never charged, then used torture to raise his sentence to its maximum.

What happened

The charge sheet accused Cadet Sanopao of one thing: violating Article of War 97 because he "instigated or ordered" the maltreatment of Cadet Dormitorio. Torture was never alleged.

The court knew it. When it came to the penalty, it refused to apply the Anti-Torture Act's own mandatory penalty for exactly that reason:

"… cannot be imposed if the same were not alleged sufficiently in the common Specifications Nr 1. 'The rule is that qualifying circumstances must be properly pleaded in the Information in order not to violate the accused's constitutional right to be properly informed of the nature and cause of the accusation against him.'"Joint Decision, pp. 90–91

One paragraph later it did the opposite. It took the penalty for homicide and raised it to the maximum period under Section 22 of the same Act, a provision that applies only when a crime is "attended by any of the acts constituting torture." The acts it named as torture, the "barkadahan" and the kicking while hanging, were done by others, not by Cadet Sanopao (Joint Decision, pp. 91–92).

The dispositive then declared his conduct "tantamount to homicide by acts of torture, pursuant to Article 249 of the Revised Penal Code in conjunction with Section 22 of the Anti-Torture Act of 2009" (p. 92).

Even the arithmetic does not hold. The reasoning calls for the maximum period "notwithstanding the provisions of the Indeterminate Sentence Law" (p. 91), which would be 17 years, 4 months and 1 day to 20 years. The dispositive calls it the medium period, yet imposes 14 years, 8 months and 1 day to 20 years (p. 92). That span is neither the medium period nor a lawful indeterminate sentence.

Violated1987 Constitution, Art. III, Sec. 14(1)–(2)R.A. 9745, Sec. 22People v. Natindim, G.R. 201867 (4 Nov 2020), cited in the decision as "Natimdim"
The law

An accused has the constitutional right to be informed of the nature and cause of the accusation against him. Any circumstance that raises the penalty must itself be alleged in the charge, or it cannot be used. The court quoted this very rule from Natindim, and then did not follow it.

They may sayThe conviction is for Article of War 97, not homicide. The Revised Penal Code was only borrowed to measure the penalty.

The answerEven on that reading, the sentence fails. A circumstance that increases the penalty must be pleaded, and the court itself held that torture was not. This defect needs no weighing of evidence: it is on the face of pages 90 to 92.

02

A military charge turned into a civilian crime

The court convicted under a military article, then declared the conduct homicide with torture and imposed a civilian penalty.

What happened

Article of War 97 is the "general article." It punishes disorders and neglects prejudicial to good order and military discipline "at the discretion of such court." It names no prison term of its own, and Reclusion Temporal appears nowhere in the Articles of War.

The Manual for Courts-Martial describes the jurisdiction of courts-martial as "entirely penal or disciplinary" (Sec. 7) and confines a general court-martial to offenses "made punishable by the Articles of War" (Sec. 12). Yet the decision went further than the military offense: it declared the conduct "tantamount to homicide by acts of torture" under Article 249 of the Revised Penal Code and the Anti-Torture Act, and imposed Reclusion Temporal (Joint Decision, p. 92).

Homicide and torture are civilian crimes. Under R.A. 7055, crimes under the Revised Penal Code and special penal laws belong to the civil courts. The civil authority had in fact already examined the very same facts: on 5 June 2020 the Office of the City Prosecutor of Baguio City dismissed every charge against Cadet Sanopao for lack of probable cause.

ViolatedR.A. 7055, Sec. 1MCM, Secs. 7, 11, 12Gonzales v. Abaya, G.R. 164007 (10 Aug 2006)Navales v. Abaya, G.R. 162318 (25 Oct 2004)
The law

A court-martial's power reaches only offenses under the Articles of War. As Gonzales v. Abaya holds, a soldier may be tried under the Articles of War only "independently of, and separately from, any charges filed in the civilian courts for the same or similar acts which are penalized under the Revised Penal Code."

They may sayR.A. 7055 itself lists Article 97 among the "service-connected" offenses that a court-martial may try.

The answerAgreed, and the appeal concedes it. The court could try the military offense, and could even borrow a civilian penalty as a yardstick. What it could not do was convert that offense into homicide with torture, a crime the civilian prosecutor had already refused to charge on the same facts.

03

The acts of others were laid at his door

The prosecution said it would not prove conspiracy. Without it, only his own act counts, and his own act was about boots.

What happened

The prosecution told the court it would not prove conspiracy. That choice has a legal consequence: it can charge Cadet Sanopao only with what he himself did.

TSN 5 Feb 2021, p. 7. On 5 February 2021 the Trial Judge Advocate confirmed on the record the prosecution's earlier admission, made at the pre-trial of 6 March 2020, that it did "not intend to prove the existence of conspiracy among CDTs Sanopao, Lumbag and Imperial."

What did he do? He called Cadet Dormitorio, and the plebe's two buddies, about a missing pair of combat boots. The buddies, Cadets Imperial and Lumbag, testified that what followed that morning was physical exercise ("mase-mase") as buddy correction, and that it was their own initiative, not his order:

Yet the court charged him with the death and with blows struck by others, many of them hours later, and gave him the same sentence as the co-accused who were found to have done the acts.

ViolatedRevised Penal Code, Arts. 8 & 17(2)People v. Yanson-Dumancas, G.R. 133527-28 (1999)People v. Hirang, 803 Phil. 277 (2017)
The law

Without conspiracy, the maxim "the act of one is the act of all" does not apply: each accused answers only for his own act. A "principal by inducement" is liable only if his words were the determining cause of the crime. In Yanson-Dumancas the Supreme Court acquitted a man whose words ("take care of the two") were not "so direct, so efficacious, so powerful" as to amount to a command.

They may sayAs the senior cadet, he set the events of that day in motion.

The answerThat is conspiracy by another name, and the prosecution gave conspiracy up. Without it, the only question is whether his own words were the determining cause of the maltreatment. Words about missing boots, which the two buddies swore did not tell them to do anything, cannot be.

04

Convicted on one witness's opinion, which the court had shut out

No witness heard an order. The only link was an investigator's opinion drawn from his own cadet days, which the court had barred.

What happened

The court admitted there were "no eyewitnesses" to any order (Joint Decision, p. 72) and "no direct evidence, testimonial or otherwise" (p. 75). At summation, the Law Member put the point to the prosecutor directly:

The "inference" came through one witness: Lt. Col. Manuel Kalang-ad, the pre-trial investigating officer. He opined that calling a plebe's buddies was a way of "endorsing" him for maltreatment, and he grounded it in his own past. Under cross-examination he also described the limits of his own work:

He was never qualified as an expert. When the Law Member offered to treat him as one, the defense elected on the record to treat him only as an ordinary witness, and the Law Member then barred any further questions on his personal experience as a cadet:

The decision nonetheless relied on that very opinion, describing defense counsel as having been "amenable" to treating him as a "reliable resource person" (Joint Decision, p. 75), and gave it weight "even in the absence of any direct evidence" (p. 78).

ViolatedMCM, Sec. 78 (proof beyond reasonable doubt)Rules of Court, Rule 130 (opinion evidence)Rules of Court, Rule 133, Sec. 4 (circumstantial evidence)
The law

Guilt must rest on legal and competent evidence beyond reasonable doubt (MCM Sec. 78). Only a qualified expert may give opinion testimony, and an ordinary witness's opinion is not proof of another person's intent. The Rules also forbid building "an inference … on another inference," a rule the decision itself quotes twice (pp. 51, 76).

They may sayThe defense never moved to strike his testimony, and the Law Member ruled at summation that "the testimony of LTC KALANG-AD will stand" (TSN 18 Aug 2023, p. 15).

The answerHis testimony about what he investigated stayed in. His cadet-experience opinion did not: it was the very category the Law Member had barred on 30 June 2021. And by his own account he applied only a probable-cause test, left culpability "for the Honorable Court to decide," and took the cadets' statements "as a face-value." That cannot carry proof beyond reasonable doubt.

05

The two roommates in the room were never heard

His roommates were in the room when he spoke. The court sent them away after the prosecution called the tenor of the order "irrelevant."

What happened

Cadets Salvador and Burong were Cadet Sanopao's roommates and were in the room that morning. Cadet Salvador swore: "I can also remember that Sanopao told the buddies and Dormitorio to help each other to look for the boots. I know for sure that Sanopao DID NOT ORDER the buddies to maltreat or cause any physical harm to Dormitorio" (Affidavit, ¶¶8–10). Cadet Burong, who says he may have been dozing after morning mess (¶7), swore: "I never heard Sanopao endorse the concerned cadet for maltreatment" (¶8).

On 26 May 2023 the defense was ready to present them. The prosecution answered that the tenor of the order did not matter:

On the same day, when the defense offered another cadet to explain the academy's practices, the Law Member ruled: "Well, the prevailing practices in the PMA is not an issue here" (TSN 26 May 2023, p. 95). The court then invoked its power to stop further evidence, and the prosecution confirmed: "for LT COBRADO and LT SALVADOR, they will no longer be presented" (p. 102). Cadet Burong was never presented either. The affidavits were tendered as excluded evidence at the promulgation hearing on 23 June 2023.

At summation the Law Member asked whether there was any need to call them, "because previously there was an admission from the prosecution." The prosecutor answered: "Yes Your Honor" (TSN 18 Aug 2023, p. 12).

Violated1987 Constitution, Art. III, Sec. 14(2)MCM, Sec. 49(F) & (G)Rules of Court, Rule 133, Sec. 7 (2019)ICCPR, Art. 14(3)(e)
The law

Due process guarantees the accused a genuine opportunity to present his defense and his witnesses. A court may stop cumulative evidence, but the rule that allows it adds: "This power shall be exercised with caution." Excluding the only disinterested witnesses who were in the room when the alleged order was given removed the defense from the decisive issue in the case.

They may sayThe prosecution conceded the tenor, so the roommates had nothing left to prove.

The answerThen the concession has to be honored. The court excused the witnesses because the prosecution admitted no one heard an order to maltreat. It cannot then convict on the theory that such an order was given.

06

The judges filled the gap with their own cadet memories

Faced with no direct evidence, the court took "judicial notice" of what its members had lived through as PMA cadets.

What happened

The decision says so in its own words:

"… the Court applied the judicial notice rule in appreciating the testimony of LTC KALANG-AD as more than the majority of its members are themselves graduates of the Philippine Military Academy and had undergone the same ordeal."Joint Decision, p. 78

This was not a slip of the pen. At summation the defense pointed to the excluded testimony of Gen. Brawner, which it said showed that an "endorsement" does not necessarily lead to maltreatment. The Law Member answered by listing the judges' PMA classes:

The same Law Member had ruled three months earlier, when the defense tried to prove those practices, that "the prevailing practices in the PMA is not an issue here" (TSN 26 May 2023, p. 95). The defense was kept from proving the culture, and the court then decided the culture from memory.

ViolatedJoint Decision, p. 78MCM, Sec. 125Rules of Court, Rule 129, Sec. 2State Prosecutors v. Judge Muro, A.M. RTJ-92-876 (1994)
The law

Judicial notice lets a court accept without proof only facts that are indisputable and commonly known. Section 125 of the Manual lists what a court-martial may notice: international law and geography, constitutional and political history, official acts of government, the laws of nature and the measure of time, the organization of the Army and its regulations, general orders, and the price of issued articles. The meaning of a cadet's words is not on that list, and a judge may not decide on facts he knows only personally (Muro).

They may sayA military court may take notice of military customs.

The answerIt may notice the Army's organization, regulations and orders, which are written and beyond dispute. What an "endorsement" meant in Echo Company in September 2019 was the disputed question of the whole case. A court cannot rule that question irrelevant when the defense tries to prove it, and then answer it from its members' own cadet years.

07

The court called its own medical witnesses, with the defense silent

The two forensic witnesses on cause and time of death were called by the court, and questioned almost entirely by the judges.

What happened

On 19 May 2023 the panel called the PNP medico-legal officer, Police Maj. Nolan Genova, "pursuant to Section 75" as a witness for the court (TSN 19 May 2023, p. 2). On 2 June 2023 it did the same with the NBI's Dr. Ronald Bandonill, who testified by video link. They testified to the two decisive facts of the case: the cause of death and when the fatal injury was inflicted.

Cadet Sanopao's counsel of choice, Atty. Ma. Concepcion Santiago, was not present when PMaj. Genova testified; he was excused (p. 26) before the private counsel appeared after lunch (p. 62). At Dr. Bandonill's hearing the military defense counsel spoke only to say there were no further questions (TSN 2 Jun 2023, pp. 25–26).

0defense questions to Dr. Bandonillnearly every question came from the panel

The court's questioning did produce one answer that helps the defense: asked by a panel member, Dr. Bandonill placed the massive injury between the hospital discharge and the next morning (TSN 2 Jun 2023, p. 17; see The medicine). But the defense had no part in shaping that record.

ViolatedMCM, Secs. 45, 49 & 751987 Constitution, Art. III, Sec. 14(2)Tabuena v. Sandiganbayan, G.R. 103501-03 (17 Feb 1997)People v. Opida, G.R. L-46272 (1986)
The law

A witness for the court is still a witness the accused may confront: Section 75-C of the Manual gives defense counsel the cross-examination of witnesses for the court. The accused also keeps his right to counsel of his choice. In Tabuena the Supreme Court set aside a conviction where the judges asked far more questions than the prosecutor, because the court had "descended into the arena."

They may sayA court-martial may call its own witnesses under Section 75.

The answerIt may. But it must then let the defense cross-examine them, with the counsel the accused chose. Calling the two witnesses who fixed the time of death, examining them from the bench, and doing it while his lawyer was absent, is the descent into the arena that Tabuena forbids.

08

The presiding officer sat in judgment of his own institution

The officer who presided when the decision was handed down was also the PMA's Assistant Superintendent.

What happened

Brig. Gen. Paulo C. Teodoro sat as a member of the court during the trial, and he presided over the court that promulgated the decision (other officers presided during the trial). Throughout, he was also the Assistant Superintendent of the Philippine Military Academy.

The case turned entirely on the academy: what a PMA "endorsement" means, how the PMA hierarchy works, and what PMA barracks culture allows. The decision itself describes the court as "seizing this judicial opportunity in condemning this senseless and archaic practice" (Joint Decision, p. 78). A senior officer of that academy sat in judgment of it.

ViolatedMCM, Sec. 58(e)Dacera v. Judge Dizon, A.M. RTJ-00-1573
The law

Section 58(e) of the Manual makes a member challengeable for "any other facts indicating that he should not sit … in the interest of having the trial … free from substantial doubt as to legality, fairness, and impartiality." Litigants are entitled to "the cold neutrality of an impartial judge," and due process requires not only actual impartiality but its appearance (Dacera).

They may sayHe was only one of several members, and no actual bias has been shown.

The answerThe rule does not ask for proof of bias. It asks whether there are facts that put the fairness of the trial in substantial doubt. An academy official presiding over a judgment of that academy's culture is such a fact.

09

The pre-trial investigation was quick, and it was one-sided

The investigation that named him was ordered to be fast, relied on undated and unsworn statements, and never heard his side.

What happened

The academy's first investigator, Capt. Gamos, testified that because the death had become a "national issue," the Commandant, then Brig. Gen. Bartolome Bacarro, ordered a "mabilisang imbestigasyon," a quick investigation (TSN 5 Feb 2021, p. 13).

The formal pre-trial investigation under Article of War 71 followed. Its officer, Lt. Col. Kalang-ad, built his report on written statements that were undated and unsworn, as he admitted on the stand:

He also admitted that Cadet Sanopao was never allowed to cross-examine anyone or to present his side, and the company tactical officer admitted that the delinquency report was never given to him to explain:

ViolatedArticle of War 71MCM, Secs. 30(c) & 35(a)CCAFPR (Graybook), Sec. 70(c), (d) & (i)Cudia v. Superintendent, PMA, G.R. 211362 (24 Feb 2015)
The law

Article of War 71 and Section 35(a) of the Manual require that "full opportunity shall be given to the accused to cross-examine witnesses against him … and to present anything he may desire in his own behalf." The academy's own regulations require the duplicate delinquency report to be referred "to the reported cadet for explanation," that he "write an explanation at the reverse side," and that a grave report be forwarded "with the cadet's explanation" (CCAFPR Sec. 70). As Cudia holds, "the PMA is not immune from the strictures of due process."

They may sayThe Supreme Court has held that the Article 71 investigation is directory, not jurisdictional (Comendador v. De Villa, 1991), so a defect in it does not void the trial.

The answerThe appeal does not claim it voids the trial. The point is what the defect produced: the only investigation that named Cadet Sanopao rested on undated, unsworn statements, never heard him, and its author's opinion became the conviction's only link to an "order."

10

The burden of proof was turned upside down

A member of the same court, at the same session, asked an accused cadet to prove his own innocence.

What happened

On 12 May 2023, at the same session of GCM No. 2, the court heard the separate case of another cadet charged over the same incident. A member of the court that day, Col. Resurreccion, pressed that cadet to prove he was innocent:

The question was not put to Cadet Sanopao, and Col. Resurreccion is not among the officers who signed the Joint Decision. It is offered for what it shows: the standard at work on that bench. The same attitude surfaced elsewhere in the record:

  • At summation, when a member pressed the defense to explain why it had not rebutted the prosecution's evidence, counsel objected: "In criminal prosecution we don't pin down weaknesses of the defense evidence to establish their guilt" (TSN 18 Aug 2023, as quoted in the appeal).
  • At the arraignment, the then Law Member told him that "civilian counsels are not that competent to represent you" (TSN 14 Feb 2020, p. 37).
Violated1987 Constitution, Art. III, Sec. 14(2)MCM, Sec. 78ICCPR, Art. 14(2)UN Human Rights Committee, General Comment No. 32
The law

Every accused is presumed innocent, and the burden of proving guilt beyond reasonable doubt rests on the prosecution alone. It never shifts to the accused, and a weak defense is never a substitute for a strong prosecution.

They may sayThe question was asked of another accused, in another case.

The answerYes, and this page says so. It is not offered as an error in his own case, but as evidence of the standard the same court was applying on the same day, to the same incident.

11

The bench prompted the prosecutor against a defense witness

While a defense witness was testifying, a panel member turned to the prosecutor and supplied the objection.

What happened

On 15 September 2023, Lt. Col. Ramon Gualberto testified as a defense witness for co-accused Cadet Imperial. He had held and interviewed the detained cadets, and he was describing the loopholes in the investigation. His conclusion was blunt: "… we are very, very sure that may nangyari nung gabi" (something happened that night) (TSN 15 Sep 2023, pp. 21–22).

During that testimony, a panel member turned to the Trial Judge Advocate and prompted the objection:

ViolatedDacera v. Judge Dizon, A.M. RTJ-00-1573People v. Opida, G.R. L-46272 (1986)
The law

The Trial Judge Advocate is the prosecutor; the panel is the judge. A court rules on objections. When it supplies them to one side against a witness for the other, it has stopped being a neutral adjudicator.

They may sayA judge may ask a lawyer what a rule is called.

The answerNot in the middle of a defense witness's testimony, and not of the prosecutor alone, in a way that produces the objection the prosecutor had not made. That is taking a side.
