The appeal

Twelve grounds,one question.

On 11 June 2026, Cadet Sanopao filed his appeal with the Chief of Staff, AFP under Presidential Decree No. 1199: 34 pages, twelve grounds, and 333 pages of annexes. This page explains each ground in plain words, with what the record shows.

The controlling principle

The prosecution abandoned conspiracy.

Beneath all twelve grounds lies one fact the prosecution conceded and the court recorded: it would not prove that Cadet Sanopao agreed with, or acted together with, Cadets Imperial and Lumbag. That concession decides three things.

  1. Only his own act can be charged to him. Without conspiracy, "the act of one is the act of all" does not apply (Revised Penal Code, Art. 8). What others did, and the death, cannot be imputed to him.
  2. The conviction rests on one inference, and it is innocent. The only link to an "order" is the meaning given to an "endorsement," drawn from a witness's cadet experience that the court had shut out at trial.
  3. The death cannot be bridged to him. The fatal injury did not exist when Dormitorio was discharged after seven hours of observation. It came that evening, from other hands.

The appeal asks one question: does the record prove, beyond reasonable doubt, that he instigated or ordered maltreatment? The twelve grounds below say it does not.

Appeal to the Chief of Staff, AFP under P.D. 1199, filed 11 June 2026, Preliminary Statement and Statement of the Controlling Principle

The twelve grounds

Each ground gives the short version first, then what the record shows, with the page of the decision or transcript beside every quotation.

I The sentence is voidThe court held that torture was never charged, then used that same uncharged torture to push the penalty to its maximum.

What the record shows

In fixing the penalty, the court first refused to impose the Anti-Torture Act's mandatory penalty, because torture was never alleged in the specification:

"… cannot be imposed if the same were not alleged sufficiently in the common Specifications Nr 1. 'The rule is that qualifying circumstances must be properly pleaded in the Information in order not to violate the accused's constitutional right to be properly informed of the nature and cause of the accusation against him.'"Joint Decision, pp. 90–91

One paragraph later, it took the homicide penalty and raised it to its maximum period under Section 22 of the same Act, which applies only when a crime is "attended by any of the acts constituting torture," naming the "barkadahan" and the kicking while hanging as the torture (Joint Decision, pp. 91–92). Those acts were committed by others, not by Cadet Sanopao.

The computation is also incoherent. The body of the decision calls for the maximum period "notwithstanding the provisions of the Indeterminate Sentence Law" (p. 91), which would be 17 years 4 months 1 day to 20 years. The dispositive calls it the medium period, yet imposes 14 years 8 months 1 day to 20 years (p. 92). That span is neither the medium period nor a lawful indeterminate sentence.

Why it matters

This ground needs no weighing of evidence. Even if the conviction stood, the sentence could not.

II A military charge merged with civilian crimesThe court convicted under a military article but declared the conduct "tantamount to homicide by acts of torture" under civilian statutes.

What the record shows

The Manual for Courts-Martial says the jurisdiction of courts-martial "is entirely penal or disciplinary" (Sec. 7), and confines a general court-martial to offenses "made punishable by the Articles of War" (Sec. 12). The Supreme Court held in Gonzales v. Abaya (2006) that a soldier may be tried under the Articles of War only "independently of, and separately from, any charges filed in the civilian courts for the same or similar acts which are penalized under the Revised Penal Code."

The dispositive did not stop at the military offense. It declared the conduct "tantamount to homicide by acts of torture, pursuant to Article 249 of the Revised Penal Code in conjunction with Section 22 of the Anti-Torture Act of 2009," and imposed a civilian penalty (Joint Decision, p. 92). The civilian authority that R.A. 7055 designates had already examined the same facts and dismissed every charge against him.

Why it matters

The appeal concedes that R.A. 7055 lets a court-martial borrow a civilian penalty as a yardstick for the military offense actually proved. It does not let the court turn that offense into homicide with torture.

III No conspiracy, so no one else's actsThe prosecution said it would not prove conspiracy. Without it, only Cadet Sanopao's own act can be charged to him, and that act was about boots.

What the record shows

On 5 February 2021 the prosecution confirmed on the record its earlier admission that it did "not intend to prove the existence of conspiracy among CDTs Sanopao, Lumbag and Imperial" (TSN 5 Feb 2021, p. 7). Under Article 8 of the Revised Penal Code, without conspiracy each accused answers only for his own acts.

He was charged as one who "instigated or ordered." Under Article 17(2) of the Revised Penal Code, inducement must be the determining cause of the crime. In People v. Yanson-Dumancas (1999) the Supreme Court acquitted an alleged inducer whose words ("take care of the two") were not "so direct, so efficacious, so powerful" as to amount to a command. Cadet Sanopao's words were an instruction to find missing boots. The two cadets who supposedly received the "order" testified otherwise:

Why it matters

Strip away every act done by someone else, as the abandonment of conspiracy requires, and what remains is a cadet who called a plebe and his buddies about boots.

IV One inference, and it was incompetentThe court admitted there was no direct evidence. The only bridge to an "order" was an opinion the court itself had shut out.

What the record shows

The Manual requires guilt "established by legal and competent evidence beyond reasonable doubt" (Sec. 78), and the Rules say "an inference cannot be based on another inference," a rule the decision itself quotes twice (pp. 51, 76).

  • The court admitted there were "no eyewitnesses" to any order (p. 72) and "no direct evidence, testimonial or otherwise" (p. 75).
  • Dormitorio's own statements before his death, which the court used against the buddies (pp. 52–58), name only his buddies. They never name Cadet Sanopao.
  • The single link was Lt. Col. Kalang-ad's opinion that calling a plebe's buddies was "another way of endorsing" him, grounded in his "experience as a former cadet." The defense had elected to treat him only as an ordinary witness, and the Law Member barred questions about his cadet experience (TSN 30 Jun 2021, pp. 24–25).
  • He also told the court his own work did not apply the reasonable-doubt standard: "… we're talking of an administrative case, and in fact what I was doing was a preliminary investigation so if you want to say that I had to impose a higher level or standard of evidence like beyond reasonable doubt … I did not do that, MA'AM" (TSN 30 Jun 2021, as quoted in the appeal).

Even the prosecution's eyewitness, Cadet Sierra, said that the attention a buddy gives can be positive: "… if positive yung pagkakapansin, your Honor, positive reinforcement din …" (TSN 26 Nov 2021, as quoted in the appeal). An "endorsement" that can be a commendation cannot, alone, prove an order to maltreat.

Why it matters

Where the only link admits an innocent reading, the chain the Rules require is broken.

V The death was not caused by the morningSeven hours of hospital observation found no fresh injury. The massive injury came after the discharge, from other hands.

What the record shows

The attending physician confirmed that at discharge "all the vital signs were in order," and that the only contusion was an old one seen on 6 September (TSN 15 Jul 2021, pp. 19–20). The duty nurse saw no fresh injury, then found the body "very different" the next dawn (TSN 26 May 2023, pp. 7, 16). The defense pathologist testified that the injuries could not have been present during the observation, and the court's own NBI witness, asked by a panel member, placed the massive injury between the discharge and the next morning (see The medicine).

An independent assault by others is an efficient intervening cause that breaks the chain (Urbano v. IAC, 1988; People v. Villacorta, 2011). The court relied on People v. Lalap (p. 89), but there the accused himself struck the fatal blow. The court also found that timely medical intervention "could have saved his life" (pp. 79–88), a further cause it cannot square with blaming the morning.

Lt. Col. Ramon Gualberto, who held and interviewed the detained cadets, put it plainly: "… we are very, very sure that may nangyari nung gabi" (something happened that night) (TSN 15 Sep 2023, pp. 21–22).

Why it matters

Contribution to a death requires a contribution to the body that died. The medicine shows none from the morning.

VI The two roommates were shut outThe only first-hand witnesses besides the participants were never heard. The prosecution called what they would say "irrelevant."

What the record shows

Cadet Salvador heard the instructions: "I can also remember that Sanopao told the buddies and Dormitorio to help each other to look for the boots. I know for sure that Sanopao DID NOT ORDER the buddies to maltreat or cause any physical harm to Dormitorio" (¶¶8–10). Cadet Burong swore: "I never heard Sanopao endorse the concerned cadet for maltreatment" (¶8).

When the defense offered Salvador on the tenor of the order, the prosecution said "this would be irrelevant," the Law Member asked "As long as it pertains to the finding of the combat boots?", and the prosecution answered "Yes, your Honors" (TSN 26 May 2023, p. 101). The witnesses were sent away. The defense tendered the affidavits at the promulgation hearing on 23 June 2023 "to prove the tenor of the order of CDT SANOPAO and rule out the claim that he ordered CDTs IMPERIAL and LUMBAG to maltreat CDT DORMITORIO."

Why it matters

The charge was that he ordered maltreatment. The tenor of the only order was not a side issue. It was the issue.

VII "Judicial notice" of the judges' own pastThe court filled the evidence gap with what its members remembered from their own cadet years.

What the record shows

The decision says the court "applied the judicial notice rule in appreciating the testimony of LTC KALANG-AD as more than the majority of its members are themselves graduates of the Philippine Military Academy and had undergone the same ordeal" (p. 78).

Section 125 of the Manual lists what a court-martial may notice: international law and geography, constitutional and political history, official acts of government, the laws of nature and the measure of time, the organization of the Army and its regulations, general orders, and the price of issued articles. The meaning of a cadet's words is not on it, and a judge "may not decide on facts known to him personally" (State Prosecutors v. Muro).

Why it matters

A disputed fact cannot be noticed because the judges share the background of the institution on trial.

VIII The president of the court and the academyThe officer who presided over the decision was also the academy's Assistant Superintendent.

What the record shows

Brig. Gen. Paulo C. Teodoro sat as a member throughout the trial and presided over the court that deliberated and promulgated the decision, while also serving as Assistant Superintendent of the Philippine Military Academy. The case turned entirely on the meaning of a PMA "endorsement," PMA hierarchy and PMA barracks culture.

Section 58(e) of the Manual makes a member challengeable for "any other facts indicating that he should not sit … in the interest of having the trial … free from substantial doubt as to legality, fairness, and impartiality."

Why it matters

Due process requires not only actual impartiality but its appearance (Dacera v. Judge Dizon).

IX Pre-trial rights deniedNo cross-examination, no defense evidence, and no copy of the report that started it all.

What the record shows

Article of War 71 and Section 35(a) of the Manual require that "full opportunity shall be given to the accused to cross-examine witnesses against him … and to present anything he may desire in his own behalf." The investigating officer admitted he allowed neither ("None." / "No Your Honor.", TSN 5 Feb 2021, pp. 47–48). The tactical officer admitted Cadet Sanopao was never given a copy of the delinquency report, never given the 24-hour period to explain, and never told of the recommendation (TSN 19 Nov 2021, pp. 50–51).

The first investigation was ordered to be quick: Capt. Gamos testified that the Commandant ordered a "mabilisang imbestigasyon" because the death had become a "national issue" (TSN 5 Feb 2021, p. 13).

Why it matters

As the Supreme Court held in Cudia, "the PMA is not immune from the strictures of due process."

X Court witnesses, with no defense questionsThe panel called its own medico-legal witnesses and asked almost every question. The defense asked none.

What the record shows

On 19 May 2023 the court called PMaj. Nolan Genova "as a witness for the court," and on 2 June 2023 Dr. Ronald Bandonill. Cadet Sanopao's counsel of choice was not present. Of at least 181 questions to PMaj. Genova, the panel asked 176; of 76 to Dr. Bandonill, the panel asked 70. The defense asked none of either.

Section 75-C of the Manual says defense counsel conducts the cross-examination of witnesses for the court. In Tabuena v. Sandiganbayan the Supreme Court set aside a conviction where the judges asked far more questions than the prosecutor.

Why it matters

A tribunal that conducts the examination itself has left "the cold neutrality of an impartial judge."

XI The burden shifted, and the bench took sidesA panel member asked another accused cadet to prove his innocence; another prompted the prosecutor's objection against a defense witness.

What the record shows

  • Burden of proof. At the same session on 12 May 2023, in the separate case of another cadet, Col. Resurreccion asked: "paano mo kami macoconvince without reasonable doubt na hindi ikaw yung naka last touch sa ulo ni DORMITORIO?" (TSN 12 May 2023, p. 92). It was not asked of Cadet Sanopao, but it shows the standard the same court was applying.
  • Prompting the prosecution. While Lt. Col. Gualberto, a defense witness for co-accused Cadet Imperial, testified about the investigation's "loopholes," a panel member asked the prosecutor, "Ah TJA, anong tawag dun? Pagka hindi nag-sign ng sworn affidavit?" The prosecutor answered, "As mentioned earlier sir, hearsay din siya sir" (TSN 15 Sep 2023, pp. 4, 29).
  • Pressing the defense at summation. When a member pressed the defense to explain why it had not rebutted the prosecution's evidence, counsel objected: "In criminal prosecution we don't pin down weaknesses of the defense evidence to establish their guilt" (TSN 18 Aug 2023, as quoted in the appeal).
  • Counsel of choice. At the arraignment, the then Law Member told him that "civilian counsels are not that competent to represent you" (TSN 14 Feb 2020, p. 37).
Why it matters

Every accused is presumed innocent, and the burden never shifts.

XII The civilian prosecutor said noApplying the lowest standard in law, the Baguio City Prosecutor found no probable cause, and refused to charge him on the same "endorsement" theory.

What the record shows

The Joint Resolution of 5 June 2020 dismissed all charges against Cadet Sanopao for lack of probable cause: "As for Sanopao, there is not an evidence presented that he did endorse Dormitorio to both Lumbag and Imperial for beating" (p. 55). When the complainants moved for reconsideration, the panel of prosecutors refused: "justice for a fallen cadet does not mean an indictment that is free-for-all. The law may be harsh, but it is the law" (Joint Review Resolution dated 24 September 2020, p. 4).

The special panel that later acted on the trial court's order amended the murder Information to name only Cadets Imperial and Lumbag (Supplemental Resolution, 12 October 2020). Cadet Sanopao was named in no Information.

Why it matters

If a prosecutor could not find even a reasonable ground of suspicion, the court-martial could not find guilt beyond reasonable doubt on the same facts.

Words you will meet

A short glossary.

The case turns on academy terms that outsiders rarely hear. These are the meanings given in the sworn record.

4CL / 3CL / 1CL
Fourth, third and first class: the cadet year levels. A 4CL cadet is a first-year "plebe"; a 1CL cadet is in the final year. Cadet Sanopao was 1CL; Cadet Dormitorio was 4CL; Cadets Imperial and Lumbag were 3CL.
Buddy
The upper-class cadet assigned to guide and correct a particular plebe. Cadets Imperial and Lumbag were Dormitorio's buddies.
Endorsement
When a plebe breaks a rule, the upper-class cadet who caught him informs the plebe's buddy "to either have the endorsed cadet comply or to subject the endorsed cadet to mase-mase or physical exercises but NOT maltreatment" (affidavit of a squad cadet, as quoted in the appeal). The court read an "endorsement" as encouraging maltreatment.
Mase-mase
Authorized corrective physical exercise, such as squat thrusts. Cadets Imperial and Lumbag testified that what they had Dormitorio do on the morning of 17 September was mase-mase as buddy correction.
Katay / pangangatay
Prohibited physical maltreatment. It is what the charge accused Cadet Sanopao of ordering, and what no witness heard him order.
"My man, my lord"
Cadet idiom for a close upper-class/plebe relationship. Cadet Salvador swore that Dormitorio "liked it better when Sanopao calls him to our room," and that "[w]hen Dormitorio is called, in effect, he is protected from endorsement" (Salvador affidavit, ¶6).
Delinquency Report (DR)
The academy's written report of an offense. The DR of 18 September 2019 described the offense only as "encouraging maltreatment" by "endorsing" Dormitorio, and the tactical officer admitted it was never furnished to Cadet Sanopao.
Article of War 97
The "general article": disorders and neglects prejudicial to good order and military discipline, "punished at the discretion of such court." It names no prison term of its own.
Reclusion Temporal
A civilian penalty under the Revised Penal Code, 12 years and 1 day to 20 years. It appears nowhere in the Articles of War.
TSN
Transcript of Stenographic Notes: the official word-for-word record of each hearing. Page numbers on this site are the transcript's own page numbers.
Reviewing authority
The Chief of Staff, AFP, who must expressly approve a court-martial sentence before it can be carried out (Joint Decision, p. 93; MCM Sec. 87).
P.D. 1199
The 1977 decree under which the appeal was filed. It created a Court of Military Appeals with the rank of the Court of Appeals; that court has never been organized.

What the appeal asks

The prayer.

  1. Reverse the Joint Decision and acquit him, for failure to prove guilt beyond reasonable doubt.
  2. In the alternative, vacate the sentence as void.
  3. Declare void the finding of conduct "tantamount to homicide by acts of torture," as beyond the court-martial's jurisdiction.
  4. Recognize that, without conspiracy, the acts of others and the death cannot be attributed to him.
  5. Strike down the conviction for the due process violations shown.
  6. Note the conflict of interest of the court's president.
  7. Give due weight to the civilian dismissal.
  8. Restore his pay and allowances.
  9. Order his release, and clear his military and academic record.
